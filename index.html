Interactive Sinhala Text Reader for the Visually Impaired

Using OCR, NLP and AI

A smartphone reader that guides a blind user to photograph a printed Sinhala newspaper, corrects the mistakes that text recognition makes, reads the article aloud, and then answers spoken questions about it in the way each listener prefers.

Literature survey

The World Health Organization estimates that at least 2.2 billion people live with a near or distance vision impairment. In Sri Lanka most people read Sinhala, yet the assistive readers in common use, such as JAWS and Google Lookout, are built around English, and Google's on-device text recognition does not support the Sinhala script at all.

Sinhala is hard for recognition engines. It is an abugida in which small vowel signs sit above, below, before or after a consonant, and touching conjuncts are common. Those small marks are the first to disappear when a photograph is slightly blurred or too far away. Published accuracy figures are mostly measured on clean synthetic text: one engine reached 0.76% character error on synthetic Sinhala but 8.84% on real legislative pages.

Sinhala OCR

Recent work moved straight to end-to-end neural models. Topology-aware preprocessing for stylised headline fonts has not been evaluated.

Post-OCR correction

A mature field for European languages. Sinhala correction work is rule- and dictionary-based, and many OCR errors form another valid Sinhala word.

Retrieval-augmented answers

Rarely treats the amount of retrieved context as a personal setting, and rarely addresses Sinhala news or answers that are heard rather than read.

Voice interaction

Sinhala speech research stops at recognition. Intent detection depends on labelled data, and assistants give every user the same response.

Capture without sight

Studies of blind users found that 84% of failed document photos were taken too close, and that short spoken directions work better than detailed feedback.

Research gap

Each strand of research covers part of the picture, but none joins the parts. There is no Sinhala-specific pipeline that carries a hand-held photograph of a printed page through accurate recognition and correction to grounded, spoken answers that adapt to the individual listener.

Research gap by component
ComponentWhat is missing in existing work
Headline recognitionNo evaluation of Medial Axis Transform skeletonisation ahead of an LSTM engine for stylised Sinhala typography
Body-text correctionNo context-aware, transformer-based post-OCR corrector for Sinhala, and no benchmark of real photographed Sinhala to compare methods on
Answer generationNo style-aware retrieval for Sinhala news in which the user's preferred style controls both wording and retrieval depth
Voice and personalisationNo Sinhala assistive voice interface that learns each user's answer style online, without a labelled dataset

Research problem

Sinhala-speaking visually impaired and low-vision people cannot independently obtain, on demand, the content of printed Sinhala newspapers in a form they can trust and follow by ear.
  • No available tool serves the need. Commercial readers do not offer Sinhala, and academic prototypes pass raw images straight to standard engines.
  • Open-source recognition is not accurate enough to listen to. On newspaper pages photographed under realistic conditions, Tesseract made a character error rate of 0.1197 and a word error rate of 0.3358, so about one spoken word in three was wrong.
  • Errors are hard to detect. A listener hears a wrong word in the same confident voice as a right one.
  • Existing tools cannot be asked anything. They read whole articles in one fixed style and cannot summarise, explain or simplify.

Research objectives

Main objective. Design, build and evaluate an integrated smartphone-based Sinhala reader that lets visually impaired and low-vision users capture a printed newspaper page without sight, have its headline and body text recognised and corrected, ask about it in spoken Sinhala, and hear grounded answers in the style each user prefers.

Specific objectives

  1. Enable eyes-free capture of a newspaper page at the resolution recognition needs.
  2. Isolate the article's headline and body without clipping Sinhala vowel signs.
  3. Recognise stylised Sinhala headlines and evaluate topological preprocessing.
  4. Reduce body-text recognition errors to a level a listener can follow.
  5. Generate grounded Sinhala answers about the current article, in the requested style.
  6. Accept spoken Sinhala requests and learn each user's answer style without an upfront dataset.
  7. Integrate the four components so that the failure of one does not stop reading.
  8. Validate the integrated system with intended users.

Methodology

The system is one reading pipeline. Each numbered layer receives a shared article object, adds to it and passes it on, and the layer boundary is also the ownership boundary between the four members. The components run as separate services joined by HTTP and JSON contracts, so a component that is down switches off one feature instead of stopping the reader.

C1

Topological Sinhala Character Recognition via Medial Axis Transform

M.J.J Muthunayaka

Isolates the article with PaddleOCR layout detection and CRAFT line detection, then reads the headline through two branches in parallel: a raw binarised branch and a Medial Axis Transform skeleton branch, each with its own custom Tesseract LSTM model trained on 90,000 SinOCR images.

The raw branch is the stronger general pipeline (mean CER 0.0586 against 0.0922 on 8,872 images); skeletonisation wins on 29 of 135 complex fonts.

C2

Context-Aware Sinhala Post-OCR Error Correction Model

H.P.I Madhusanka

Guides capture to a target glyph height with an automatic shutter, recognises the body with Tesseract, and corrects each sentence with an mT5-small model trained on synthetic errors drawn from a measured taxonomy of 192 Sinhala character confusions.

Character errors fell by 36.7% and word errors by 51.2% on a locked test set; listeners' word recovery rose from 60.0% to 79.2%.

C3

Advanced Document Retrieval and Information Generation

W.G.N. Devindi

Splits about 500 Sinhala newspaper articles into paragraphs, indexes them with dense and BM25 retrieval fused by reciprocal rank, and generates a Sinhala answer whose wording and depth follow the requested style.

On 72 test queries, 90.2% of answers were faithful to the evidence and 93.8% of unanswerable questions were correctly declined.

C4

Adaptive Conversational Personalization and Voice Interaction

Sathsara S.P.Y.B

Translates the spoken Sinhala request with NLLB-200, detects the intent with a local Llama 3.2 1B model, and decides the answer style through a four-tier rule that ends in a per-user online model learning only from the user's own confirmed preferences.

Identified the intent of 35 of 45 unseen Sinhala commands (77.78%) at 1.53 s each, with no Sinhala intent dataset.

Technologies used

Mobile client

  • Kotlin, CameraX, Retrofit
  • Android speech recognition (si-LK)
  • Android TextToSpeech, Google Sinhala voice

Reader service

  • Python, FastAPI, uvicorn
  • Pydantic schema contracts
  • pytest (325 cases)

Vision and OCR

  • Tesseract 5 with custom LSTM models
  • PaddleOCR, CRAFT, OpenCV
  • scikit-image (Zhang-Suen thinning)

Language models

  • mT5-small (Hugging Face Transformers, PyTorch)
  • NLLB-200, Llama 3.2 1B via llama-cpp-python
  • gpt-5.4-mini through the OpenAI API

Retrieval and learning

  • LangChain, ChromaDB, rank_bm25
  • multilingual-e5-small embeddings
  • River online learning, TinyDB

Development

  • Git with per-member branches
  • Google Colab (T4) for training
  • NVIDIA RTX 4060 workstation
